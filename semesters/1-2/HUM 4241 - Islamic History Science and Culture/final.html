1 If Al-Khwarizmi's "al-jabr" shaped algebra, how does his legacy live on every time you use an algorithm in coding? ▾
Answer

Al-Khwarizmi developed the discipline of algebra in his work al-jabr, which gave algebra its name. His step-by-step methods for solving equations also live on in the word algorithm, derived from the Latin form of his name. The course material connects his mathematical legacy with computing by noting that every computer program owes something to his work.

Whenever a programmer follows a systematic sequence of steps to solve a problem, the legacy of Al-Khwarizmi's method is reflected in the use of algorithms. His contribution shows how mathematical ideas developed during the Islamic Golden Age became the basis for later scientific and technological work. Algebra provided systematic methods for calculation and problem-solving, while his algorithmic approach shows how ordered, logical procedures are central to computation.

Al-Khwarizmi al-jabr → Algebra Algorithm (Latin latinisation) Islamic Golden Age
2 Al-Zahrawi's surgical tools are still in use; what does that tell us about the importance of design durability in engineering? ▾
Answer

Al-Zahrawi established the foundation of modern surgery and developed many innovative surgical instruments and techniques. The course material lists instruments such as the syringe, forceps, surgical hook and needle, bone saw, and lithotomy scalpel, and notes that many of his instruments and techniques are still used today. This demonstrates that an engineering design can remain useful when it successfully addresses the needs for which it was created.

For engineers, durability means producing designs that are not only innovative but also reliable and useful over time. Al-Zahrawi's work shows that careful design can have a lasting influence when it combines practical function with sound construction and serves an important human need.

Al-Zahrawi Syringe · Forceps · Bone Saw Design Durability Father of Modern Surgery

4 How might Ibn al-Haytham's experimental approach to optics inspire engineers in designing today's cameras and sensors? ▾
Answer

Ibn al-Haytham is described as the Father of Modern Optics and as an early experimental physicist. He insisted that scientific claims should be supported by empirical proof and experimentation. In his Book of Optics, he explained how vision occurs and showed that light enters the eye. He also investigated the camera obscura phenomenon.

This experimental approach provides engineers with an important model: optical designs should be based on observation, testing, evidence, and careful understanding of how light behaves. Cameras and sensors depend on the controlled study and use of light, so Ibn al-Haytham's emphasis on experimentation and his discoveries in optics provide an important foundation for developing and improving such technologies.

Ibn al-Haytham Book of Optics Camera Obscura Father of Modern Optics
5 Al-Biruni estimated Earth's radius within 50 km accuracy — how could his triangulation methods guide modern satellite engineering? ▾
Answer

Al-Biruni established techniques for measuring the Earth and distances using triangulation, contributing greatly to geography and geodesy. He solved complex geodesic equations and estimated the Earth's radius at 6,339.6 kilometres, less than 50 kilometres short of the modern value. His work demonstrates how careful measurement, geometry, mathematical calculation, and systematic observation can produce highly accurate results.

Modern satellite engineering similarly depends on accurate measurement of the Earth and geographical positions. Al-Biruni's method provides an example of how geometric measurement and mathematical precision can support work involving the Earth's shape, distances, and location. His achievement also demonstrates that sophisticated engineering results can be obtained through disciplined methods even with limited instruments.

Al-Biruni Triangulation 6,339.6 km Earth Radius Geodesy

7 If Umar (R.A) could design a 69-mile canal to transport food during famine, what modern engineering project could you compare this with in terms of crisis management? ▾
Answer

During a severe famine in Arabia, Umar (R.A) ordered a 69-mile canal to be dug from the Nile to the Red Sea. When the work was completed, twenty ships carrying corn travelled from the Nile to the Red Sea and safely reached the port of Madinah. The project demonstrates engineering being used directly to respond to an urgent humanitarian crisis by creating a transportation route for essential supplies.

A modern comparison can be made with a large emergency infrastructure project designed to restore or provide essential supply routes during a crisis. The important lesson is not simply the size of the project but its purpose: identifying an urgent need, developing practical infrastructure, and delivering essential resources to affected people. It shows how engineering can support crisis management and public welfare.

Umar (RA) 69-Mile Canal Nile → Red Sea Crisis Engineering
8 How different would cameras and fiber-optic technology be without Ibn al-Haytham's optics discoveries? ▾
Answer

Ibn al-Haytham made major contributions to optics by explaining that humans see when light reflected from objects enters the eye. He rejected earlier theories that light was emitted from the eye and also investigated the camera obscura phenomenon. His work established a strong experimental foundation for understanding light and vision. Because cameras depend on the formation and control of images through light, his optical discoveries are closely relevant to the development of camera technology.

The course material specifically presents optics as one of the major Muslim contributions that shaped the modern world. His experimental treatment of light provided a systematic way of understanding optical phenomena. Technologies based on optical imaging and the controlled use of light can be viewed in the context of the foundations established by Ibn al-Haytham.

14 If Al-Ma'mun's "House of Wisdom" was a hub of global knowledge exchange, what would its equivalent look like in today's digital age of engineering? ▾
Answer

Al-Ma'mun's Bayt al-Hikmah (House of Wisdom) was a state-sponsored academy, library, and centre of translation. Scholars of different backgrounds were welcomed, and works from Sanskrit, Greek, Syriac, and Persian traditions were translated into Arabic. This created an environment where knowledge from different cultures could be collected, preserved, translated, discussed, and developed further.

Its modern equivalent would be a large digital knowledge network where engineers and researchers from different countries and disciplines can share scientific knowledge, collaborate, translate important material, and build new work on earlier discoveries. The essential principle is the same: knowledge should be preserved, made accessible, and used as a foundation for further innovation.

Bayt al-Hikmah Multi-language Translation Open Knowledge Network Global Collaboration

26 When Al-Ma'mun's astronomers measured the Earth's circumference with only simple tools, what lesson does that give to engineers about precision and innovation with limited resources? ▾
Answer

Al-Ma'mun's astronomers carried out a careful geodetic operation to measure the length of a terrestrial degree and determine the size and circumference of the Earth. The course states that they obtained 563 Arabic miles for a degree of the meridian, a remarkably accurate result, using the equipment available at the time, including instruments such as the quadrant, astrolabe, dial, and globes.

The lesson for engineers is that limited resources do not necessarily prevent precise results. Careful observation, appropriate instruments, mathematical reasoning, systematic measurement, and disciplined methods can produce highly accurate outcomes. Engineering innovation therefore depends not only on having advanced technology but also on using available resources intelligently and applying rigorous methods to obtain reliable results.
